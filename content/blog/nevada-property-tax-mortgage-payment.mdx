Buyers moving to Las Vegas from California or the Midwest usually get one pleasant surprise. Property tax here tends to be lower than what they are used to paying.

It still belongs in your payment, though, and most buyers never see how the number is built. Here is the short version.

## How the Bill Is Figured

Nevada does not tax your home at its purchase price. The county assessor sets a taxable value, and property is then assessed at 35% of that taxable value. A tax rate is applied to that assessed amount.

In Clark County, combined rates commonly land somewhere around $3.20 to $3.50 per $100 of assessed value, depending on which districts your address sits in. Two homes with the same price can get different bills.

The takeaway: do not guess from the sale price. Look up the actual parcel with the Clark County Assessor before you build your budget.

## The 3% Cap on Owner-Occupied Homes

Nevada limits how fast your tax bill can rise. For a primary residence you live in, the increase is capped at 3% per year. Second homes, rentals, and other property can see increases of up to 8%.

A few details that trip people up:

- The cap applies to one primary residence per person.
- New construction does not get the cap in its first fiscal year. The cap starts the following year.
- When ownership changes, the abatement can drop off. After you close, watch for a postcard from the county and reapply so you keep the 3% cap.

## What It Looks Like in Real Numbers

Effective tax rates in most Las Vegas and Henderson neighborhoods often run somewhere around 0.5% to 0.85% of market value.

As a simple example, a $500,000 home at about 0.6% is roughly $3,000 a year, or about $250 a month. That is an illustration, not your bill. Your actual number depends on the parcel.

## How It Gets Paid

Clark County property tax is billed in installments through the year, and the schedule is set by the county. Most buyers do not pay these directly. The lender collects a monthly amount with the mortgage payment, holds it in an escrow account, and pays the county when bills come due. ## Why It Matters for Approval

Property tax is part of your monthly housing payment, so it counts in your debt-to-income ratio. A higher tax bill can lower the price range you qualify for, even if the interest rate stays the same. If you want a refresher on that math, start with [What Debt-to-Income Ratio Actually Means](/resources/blog/debt-to-income-ratio-explained).

With new construction, ask early how taxes are likely to be assessed in the first few years. The first bill can look different from the number you were quoted.
