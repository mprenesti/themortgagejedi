A special assessment is a charge on a property to pay for local improvements, and it can add real money to your housing costs. In Clark County it is also easy to miss, because it is billed separately from your property taxes.

Here is what it is, how lenders count it, and how to find one before you write an offer.

## What a Special Assessment Is

The [Clark County Treasurer](https://www.clarkcountynv.gov/government/elected_officials/county_treasurer/understanding-sids) explains that a special assessment is a charge levied against properties inside a Special Improvement District, often called an SID. Typical improvements include streets, sidewalks, curbs, streetlights, and sewer and water facilities.

The assessment is a lien on the property until it is paid off. Under [NRS Chapter 271](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-271.html#NRS271Sec420), Nevada cities, counties, and towns can form these districts, and the payments are secured by that lien.

The payments cover principal and interest, and they repay the bonds that funded the project. That is why the Treasurer treats late payments seriously.

## It Is Not Part of Your Property Tax Bill

The Treasurer says special assessments are different from real property taxes and are billed separately. They are typically billed and collected twice a year.

That matters when you estimate costs. If you use the seller's property tax bill to guess your payment, an SID assessment will not be in that number. My post on [Nevada property taxes](/resources/blog/nevada-property-taxes-explained) covers what the regular bill includes, and an assessment is a separate cost on top of it.

An HOA special assessment is a different thing. That is a charge from your association, not from a government district. My post on [HOA fees and what you qualify for](/resources/blog/las-vegas-hoa-fees-mortgage) explains how lenders treat those.

## What It Can Do to Your Payment

Here is a hypothetical example. Say a home carries an SID assessment of $1,200 every six months. That is $2,400 a year, or about $200 a month.

- On a gross monthly income of $8,000, an extra $200 adds 2.5 points to your debt-to-income ratio.
- At a 6.75% rate, $200 a month of payment room is worth about $30,800 of loan amount, counting principal and interest only.

These numbers are for illustration, not a quote. A modest-looking assessment can still reduce the price range you qualify for, because lenders count it as a housing cost.

## How Lenders Count It

Fannie Mae's [Selling Guide](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-03/monthly-housing-expense-subject-property) includes special assessments in the monthly housing expense used to calculate debt-to-income. It also says that if an assessment [was not paid at closing](https://guide-selling.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.5-04/escrow-accounts), the borrower's payment must include accruals so the estimated annual amount is collected by the time it comes due.

[HUD's FHA guidance](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/17-06ML.PDF) also lists special assessments among the items a lender collects in escrow. Rules vary by loan program and lender, so ask yours early.

In practice, you will see one of two outcomes. The assessment gets paid off at closing, or it gets counted in your monthly payment and your qualifying. My post on [escrow accounts](/resources/blog/escrow-account-payment-change) shows how taxes and insurance flow into your payment, and an assessment can add to what your lender collects.

## How to Find Out Before You Make an Offer

Do not wait for the last week of escrow. Here is the order I would use.

1. **Ask the listing agent in writing.** Ask whether the property sits in a special improvement district or carries any assessments.
2. **Look at the preliminary title report.** It is ordered when escrow opens. Nevada law provides for [recording the assessment roll](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-271.html#NRS271Sec420), so a title search will typically pick up an assessment lien.
3. **Read the Seller's Real Property Disclosure.** Under [NRS 113.130](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-113.html#NRS113Sec130), the seller must serve it at least 10 days before closing. That is late if you are still deciding on an offer, so ask your questions first.
4. **Contact the Clark County Treasurer.** Ask whether the parcel is in an SID, the remaining balance, and the payment schedule. Cities can also run their own districts, so check with the local city if the property sits inside one.

## Questions to Ask Your Lender

Once you know an assessment exists, bring these to your loan officer.

- How will this assessment be counted in my debt-to-income ratio?
- Will it be paid off at closing, or carried in my payment?
- Does my loan program have its own rule for assessments with a long time remaining?
- How much does it change the price range I qualify for?

Get the answers before your inspection period ends, while you still have room to renegotiate.

## What Happens If You Fall Behind

The Treasurer says delinquent installments carry a 2% penalty per month on the unpaid balance plus accrued interest. The county can begin foreclosure proceedings within 60 days of a delinquency.

That is a stricter outcome than most people expect from a billing line. It is the reason to know about an assessment before you own the property.

## Negotiating It

If you find an assessment, you have options. The seller can pay off the balance at closing, you can ask for a credit, or you can take it on and pay it over time.

Payoff terms are set by each district. Under NRS 271, prepayment rules depend on the ordinance that created the assessment, so ask for the remaining balance and whether paying it off early has a cost. Any seller credit also has to fit within your loan's limits on seller contributions. My post on [closing costs](/resources/blog/closing-costs-truth) explains how credits work.

Confirm the details with your title company or an attorney. I can explain how a lender counts the assessment, but I do not give legal advice on the lien itself.
